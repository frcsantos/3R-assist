## Assert3R Glossary

**Animal counts:** Structured number of animals extracted from a submitted protocol: **Females**, **Males**, **Total**, **Per group**. Only subfields explicitly stated in the protocol text are populated; derived values are prohibited. Unset when the protocol has no meaningful experimental animal cohort (e.g. slaughterhouse byproducts, such as post-slaughter bovine corneas used in EVEIT).

**Animal use:** How a catalogue method uses animals or animal-derived materials — a single controlled-vocabulary value used for curation, filtering, and result-card display. Values:
- **None** — no animals and no animal-derived materials are used.
- **Animal-derived material** — products obtained from animals (sera, antibodies, enzymes, …) are used, without animals being killed for this method's tissue harvest.
- **Slaughterhouse byproduct** — tissues/organs from animals already slaughtered for food or other primary purposes (e.g. post-slaughter bovine corneas in EVEIT).
- **Animals killed for tissue** — animals are killed specifically to obtain tissue for the method.
- **Live animals** — living animals are used in the procedure.
- **Mixed or variable** — more than one of the above clearly applies, or use varies.

May be unclassified during curation when the source document does not clearly support a classification; unclassified never means “None”.

**Application:** The intended use or purpose of a study or method. Possible values: basic research, translational/applied research, regulatory use, routine production, education and training, environmental protection, species preservation, forensic inquiry, other. Formerly called “study domain”.

**Endpoint:** In toxicology and pharmacology, this refers to the biological effect or measurable parameter that a study aims to evaluate—the response that the test captures, not the substance being tested or the method itself. This is the accepted terminology in regulatory ecotoxicology (OECD, ECHA, and ANVISA use the term in this sense).
Examples of endpoints: eye irritation, skin corrosion, skin sensitization (allergenicity), genotoxicity, acute toxicity, phototoxicity, pyrogenicity. Each answers a distinct regulatory question (e.g., “Does this substance corrode the skin?” → endpoint = skin corrosion).

**Jurisdiction:** The regulatory scope in which a method is recognized or accepted. One entry per method and jurisdiction: Brazil, EU, US, OECD. Displayed as jurisdiction chips on result cards, each with a regulatory citation.

**Method:** A discrete, validatable technique that produces measurable data for a single endpoint category—e.g., TG 439 EpiSkin produces a cell viability value that classifies skin irritation. 

**Methodology:** An experimental design or broader strategy that answers a research question, potentially combining or sequencing multiple methods—e.g., an IATA (Integrated Approach to Testing and Assessment) that combines in silico + in vitro + in vivo confirmation, or a comprehensive behavioral study design.

**Route:** Describes how the test substance comes into contact with the biological system—not the type of biological system. In surrogate methods, it refers to which routes in the original protocol this method is compatible with to replace—when it is compatible with any route, no specific route is recorded.

**Test system:** The platform in which an assay is conducted — the assay modality — distinct from route, which describes how the substance comes into contact with the system. One or more of the following may apply: in silico, in chemico, in vitro, ex vivo, in vivo, hybrid, unclear.

## Animal Experimentation Ethics Glossary

**21st-Century Toxicology:** A modern approach to chemical safety assessment that seeks to replace animal testing with more advanced methods, such as in vitro, in silico and in chemico approaches.

**3Rs (Replacement, Reduction and Refinement):** Fundamental principles of ethical animal experimentation aimed at replacing, reducing and refining the use of animals in research and teaching activities.

**Adverse Outcome Pathway (AOP):** A biological sequence describing how a chemical substance can lead to an adverse effect, from the initial interaction through to consequences in the organism.

**Alternative Method:** Any method that can be used to replace, reduce or refine the use of animals in research activities.

**Analgesia:** Use of medications or techniques to relieve or eliminate pain in animals.

**Animal Welfare:** The state of an individual in relation to its attempts to cope with the environment. It involves a range of coping mechanisms, including cellular, physiological and behavioral mechanisms, encompassing conditions from the absence of suffering to the presence of positive states. (Broom, 2023).

**Arouca Law (Law No. 11,794/2008):** The Brazilian law that regulates the use of animals in teaching and research activities, establishing standards to safeguard animal welfare and creating Concea.

**ARRIVE Guidelines:** Guidelines developed to ensure that studies involving animals are reported completely and transparently, improving reproducibility and research quality.

**Cell Line:** A population of cells derived from an original tissue that is capable of reproducing indefinitely in the laboratory while maintaining specific genetic characteristics. Used in scientific research to study biological processes and test substances, reducing the need for whole organisms in many cases.

**CEUA (Ethics Committee on Animal Use):** A mandatory body in institutions that conduct teaching or research involving animals, responsible for evaluating and approving projects and ensuring compliance with ethical and legal guidelines.

**Cognitive Bias:** A systematic deviation in information processing that leads to judgments or decisions that are not fully rational. This bias arises from cognitive limitations, emotional influences or mental shortcuts, distorting the perception of reality.

**Concea (National Council for Control of Animal Experimentation):** The body responsible for overseeing teaching and research activities involving animals, ensuring compliance with ethical guidelines and the introduction of alternative methods.

**Concea Normative Resolution (RN):** Documents that establish specific guidelines for the use of animals in teaching and research activities, ensuring application of the 3Rs.

**Confirmation Bias:** The tendency of researchers to seek, interpret and remember information that confirms their hypotheses while ignoring or minimizing data that contradict them.

**Culture of Care:** A concept involving the creation of an institutional culture focused on promoting animal welfare in research and teaching, integrating actions that minimize suffering and foster respect for animals.

**Cumulative Pain Framework (CPF):** An analytical method that measures the pain experienced by animals over time, helping to understand the impact of physical and psychological suffering in different contexts (Alonso & Schuck-Paim, 2021).

**Data Science:** An interdisciplinary field that uses scientific methods, processes, algorithms and systems to extract knowledge and insights from data.

**Epidemiological Study:** An investigation of the distribution, patterns and determinants of health problems in populations, identifying risk factors in order to develop disease prevention and control strategies.

**Epistemological Bias:** In the context of research, refers to the distortion that occurs when underlying beliefs or paradigms influence the choice of or preference for certain methodologies.

**Ethical Limit:** The point at which an action or procedure should be stopped on moral grounds in order to avoid causing unjustified harm or suffering to a being. In animal research, this limit is reached when the impact on welfare cannot be justified by the potential scientific benefits.

**Ethics:** A field of study that investigates the principles and values that guide human actions, especially with regard to the impact of decisions on the beings affected by them.

**Ethology:** The scientific study of behavior, focused primarily on observing individuals in their natural environments. Ethology investigates how behaviors are acquired, developed and evolved, considering genetic factors and environmental influences. This field encompasses behaviors such as feeding, reproduction, social interactions, learning and survival strategies, and is closely related to welfare and cognition.

**Euthanasia:** The act of inducing an animal's death in a controlled and painless manner, with the aim of preventing or ending extreme and irreversible suffering, in accordance with ethical and legal standards.

**Experimental Design:** The structure or plan of an experiment that specifies how data will be collected and analyzed, with the aim of ensuring that hypotheses can be tested rigorously.

**Fallacy:** Reasoning or an argument that appears valid but contains a logical error that makes it incorrect. In scientific and ethical contexts, fallacies can distort the interpretation of data or justify inappropriate practices, such as the naturalistic fallacy, which confuses what is natural with what is morally acceptable.

**FELASA (Federation of European Laboratory Animal Science Associations):** A European organization that promotes guidelines and good practices for the care and welfare of animals used in experimentation.

**Gavage:** A forced-feeding method used to administer substances directly into an animal's stomach through a tube.

**Grimace Scale:** A scale used to assess pain levels in animals through changes in facial expressions.

**Humane Endpoint:** The point at which a research intervention should be stopped to prevent excessive suffering in an animal. This point is determined by criteria indicating intolerable pain, suffering or distress and is intended to ensure ethical and humane action, such as euthanasia. Although euthanasia may be one action taken, a humane endpoint refers to the cessation of suffering, not necessarily to ending the individual's life.

**Humane Limit:** A measure that defines the acceptable level of suffering in animal experiments, establishing criteria for stopping a procedure before suffering exceeds the permitted level.

***in chemico:*** Methods that use chemical reactions to predict biological interactions without the use of living systems.

***in silico:*** Testing methods performed through computer simulations.

**Instrumental Relationship:** A relationship between two individuals, or between an individual and an object, in which one is used as a means to achieve a desired end. In the context of human-animal relationships, an instrumental relationship occurs when animals are not valued for their own sake but for their usefulness in achieving human goals, such as in scientific experiments, food production or other activities.

**Instrumental Value:** The value attributed to a being or object based on its usefulness in achieving a desired objective or end. In the context of animals, instrumental value means that animals are used as means to human ends, such as food production, scientific research or entertainment.

**Intrinsic Value:** The value a being possesses in itself, independently of its usefulness to others. In the case of animals, intrinsic value recognizes that they have moral value of their own and deserve respect and protection regardless of their relationship to human interests.

**Invasiveness:** The degree to which a procedure or technique interferes with an individual's state, potentially causing pain, stress or injury. In scientific research, invasiveness is a critical factor in determining the level of impact on animal welfare and is classified from mild to severe according to the severity and duration of the procedure.

***in vitro:*** Tests performed on cells or tissues cultured in the laboratory, outside a living organism.

**In Vivo Experiments:** Experiments conducted in living organisms, including animals, to observe biological reactions directly and in a natural context.

**Meta-analysis:** A research technique that combines data from several previous studies to reach a more robust conclusion and avoid unnecessary repetition of animal experiments.

**Microdosing:** Administration of small doses of substances, generally pharmaceuticals, with the aim of obtaining minimal therapeutic effects or studying biological responses without severe side effects.

**Moral Considerability:** Refers to the ethical consideration given to an individual based on the moral value attributed to that individual. This consideration may range from purely instrumental value to intrinsic value.

**NAMs (New Approach Methodologies):** Any technology, method, approach, or combination thereof that can be used to provide information on chemical hazard and risk assessment without the need to conduct new animal testing (USEPA, 2018).

**Organoids:** Small three-dimensional structures cultured in the laboratory that replicate organ functions and are used as alternatives to animal use in research.

**Organ-on-Chip:** A technology that uses microdevices containing human cells to simulate organs and predict biological responses without the need to use animals in experiments.

**PREPARE:** Guidelines for the appropriate planning of experiments involving animals, aimed at reducing errors and promoting animal welfare during research.

**Reduction:** A strategy to decrease the number of animals used in experiments without compromising scientific quality. It includes the use of improved experimental designs and statistical methods to optimize the data obtained.

**Refinement:** Improvement of procedures to minimize pain, suffering and stress in animals used in research, thereby promoting their welfare.

**Replacement:** The use of alternative techniques to avoid the use of animals in scientific experiments or teaching. Replacement may be absolute (no use of animals) or relative (use of species of lower complexity).

**Scientific Merit:** The value or quality of research based on criteria such as methodological rigor, relevance, innovation and contribution to knowledge. In animal studies, scientific merit is evaluated to ensure that the benefits of the research outweigh the potential harms to animal welfare.

**Scientific Paradigm:** A set of beliefs and methods that guide scientific practice during a given period, influencing the choice of techniques and methods used in research.

**Scientific Reproducibility:** The ability to obtain the same results in subsequent studies, which is essential for ensuring scientific validity and confidence in experimental findings.

**Sedation:** Administration of medications to reduce an animal's level of consciousness or agitation, facilitating procedures or relieving discomfort without inducing complete unconsciousness.

**Sentience:** The capacity of animals to experience pain, pleasure and other sensations or feelings, and one of the criteria considered when assessing the welfare of animals used in experimentation.

**Skin Sensitization:** A process in which the skin develops an allergic reaction after repeated exposure to a substance.

**Skin Sensitization Potency:** The degree of intensity of the allergic response that a substance can cause in the skin.

**Skin Sensitization Potential:** The ability of a substance to cause an allergic reaction in the skin.

**Social Benefit:** The positive impact that research or an intervention should provide to society in a broad and meaningful way. In the context of animal research, the social benefit must be fundamental, highly relevant and justifiable, clearly outweighing the harms and suffering caused to animals. Minor advantages or marginal contributions are not sufficient to justify animal use; the benefit should be substantial and essential to the advancement of social well-being.

**Suffering:** A negative emotional and physical state experienced by an individual, characterized by pain, discomfort, stress or distress. In animals, suffering may result from adverse conditions such as disease, injury, mistreatment, deprivation of basic needs, or scientific procedures that cause pain or stress. The concept of suffering is fundamental to discussions of animal welfare, and minimizing it through refinement of experimental and husbandry practices is essential.

**Teaching or Research Proposal Involving Animals:** A formal request submitted to a CEUA to carry out a teaching or research project involving animals, including a description of the intended protocols.

**Translatability:** The degree to which results from animal experiments can be applied or translated to humans or other species, assessing the scientific and practical relevance of such studies.

**Translational Impact:** A concept related to the practical relevance of results obtained from animal research to human or animal health, ensuring that the data generated are applicable and useful outside the laboratory setting.
